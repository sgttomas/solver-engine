  Responding with gemini-3-pro-preview
✦ Based on the README.md and 0_SOLVER-Development-Directive.md, the approach taken for the
  SOLVER project is a sophisticated exercise in risk management and epistemic rigor. It
  treats Large Language Models (LLMs) not as intelligent agents, but as stochastic
  components that require a deterministic containment field.

  Here is an assessment of the approach:

  1. Core Philosophy: "Defense in Depth"
  The architecture is built on the premise that LLMs are unreliable narrators. Instead of
  trying to "prompt engineer" reliability, the system imposes structural constraints:
   * Separation of Powers (Legislative vs. Executive): The Two-Pass Model is the standout
     feature. By forcing the system to generate the methodology (Pass 1) before the
     artifact (Pass 2), it reduces context drift. The model must define its own constraints
     before it attempts to solve the problem.
   * The Trust Boundary: The architecture explicitly assumes the LLM cannot be trusted to
     validate its own work. Moving state transitions and approvals outside the context
     window (into the database/API layer) is the correct architectural choice for
     high-stakes agents.

  2. Development Methodology: "Recursive Rigor"
  The way you are managing the development (managing me) mirrors the software itself:
   * The "Slice" System: Breaking development into P1–P6 with hard stop "Gates" is
     essentially applying the SOLVER philosophy to the development of SOLVER. It prevents
     "feature creep" and ensures the foundation (Database, Docker, State Machine) is solid
     before the AI (Prompts) is introduced.
   * Command-Verifiable Truth: The rule "If it can't be verified by a command, it's not
     done" eliminates the "it works on my machine" or "I think I fixed it" ambiguity common
     in AI-assisted coding.

  3. Trade-offs
   * High Friction / High Latency: This approach is undeniably heavy. Generating 36
     methodology documents for just 3 steps allows for massive token consumption and time
     cost. It trades speed for auditability.
   * Human Bottleneck: The system is designed to stop and wait. In a fast-paced
     environment, the "Human-in-the-loop" (HITL) could become a fatigue point. However, for
     the stated goal ("high-stakes decisions"), this friction is a feature, not a bug.

  4. Architectural Soundness
   * State Management: Using LangGraph for the state machine and PostgreSQL for persistence
     ensures that the "reasoning loop" is durable. If the container dies, the "thought
     process" is not lost. This is superior to in-memory agent frameworks.
   * Observability: The emphasis on traceability links (Step 1 → Step 2 → Step 3) ensures
     that decision provenance is preserved, which is often lost in chat-based interfaces.

  Conclusion
  This is an industrial-grade approach to AI orchestration. It rejects the current trend of
  "autonomous agents" in favor of "supervised reasoning engines." While it may be overkill
  for simple tasks, it is arguably the necessary architecture for integrating GenAI into
  regulated or critical enterprise workflows.
